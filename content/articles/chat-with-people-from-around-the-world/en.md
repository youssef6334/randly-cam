You do not need a plane ticket to hear about another person's daily life. An international chat might introduce you to a new recipe, a football rivalry or a word you have never heard. What makes it interesting is the individual in front of you, not a list of assumptions about their country.

This guide brings together greetings and conversation ideas for **22 countries**, plus practical advice about language, time zones and privacy. Use the country notes as invitations to ask, never as predictions about someone's personality. Randly is for adults **18 and over**.

## How to start an international chat on Randly
1. Open the [English version of Randly](@home) and confirm that you are an adult.
2. Choose text chat or video chat. For video, allow the browser to use your camera and microphone when prompted.
3. Select a country preference or keep the global option for a wider pool.
4. Add a few genuine interests, such as music, gaming, travel or a sport you follow.
5. Start searching. If the chosen country has few available participants, broaden the preference or try another time.

Country and interest filters help guide matching; they are not a promise of an immediate match or a verified nationality. The country flag is based on the estimated location of an internet connection. A resident abroad, a VPN or mobile-network routing can produce a flag that differs from someone's nationality or language.

Randly offers interface and text-translation options across 12 languages: Arabic, English, Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Turkish, Russian, Hindi, Indonesian and Chinese. **Text translation is not live voice interpretation.** It can make mistakes and its availability depends on the translation service. Ask which language the other person actually wants to use.

## Etiquette that travels well
Start with a greeting and one simple question. Ask permission before turning a casual chat into a language lesson. A person from France is not automatically your French tutor, just as someone from India does not represent every language spoken there.

Avoid assumptions based on appearance, name or flag. Food, music, hobbies and travel ideas are gentle starting points, but anyone can decline a topic. Salary, exact address, religion, politics and painful experiences need particular care. Do not record or publish someone else's conversation without their informed agreement.

## The Arab world: languages, greetings and ideas
Arabic includes many everyday varieties, and people may prefer another language altogether. A broadly understood “Marhaba” is a useful beginning. If a dialect word is unfamiliar, ask what it means rather than pretending to understand.

### Palestine
Try **Marhaba, kifak?** for an informal hello. Ask about a favourite dish, music or a hobby; knafeh and maqluba can be starting points if food comes up. Let the person choose whether to discuss difficult experiences. Their flag is not an invitation to question them about conflict.

### Egypt
An informal **Ezzayak?** can open an Egyptian Arabic conversation. Football, films, music and koshari offer possible questions. Instead of assuming everybody loves the same club or has the same sense of humour, ask what they personally enjoy.

### Saudi Arabia
**Marhaba** or **Hala** works as a friendly greeting. Arabic has several regional varieties here. Ask about games, travel, coffee or a sport. Be considerate if the person wants to pause for a commitment; do not guess their schedule or beliefs.

### United Arab Emirates
Say **Hala** or **Hello**, then ask which language is comfortable. The UAE has residents from many backgrounds. Food, hobbies and places worth visiting can work better than assuming the person is Emirati or asking immediately about work and income.

### Morocco
**Labas?** is a useful informal opening in Darija. Moroccan Arabic, Amazigh languages, French and other languages may be relevant to the person you meet. Ask about tea, a local dish or music, and check whether they prefer Darija, French or simple English.

### Algeria
You might hear **Wash rak?** in an informal conversation. Darija and Amazigh languages are among Algeria's languages; French may also be useful. Ask about a song, football or a landscape they recommend. Let them choose the shared language rather than assuming French.

### Kuwait
An informal **Shlonak?** may start a Gulf Arabic chat. A question about a meal, a game or a place to visit is enough. Learn a local expression if they feel like explaining it, and avoid turning dialect differences into an imitation.

### Qatar
Try **Hala** or **Marhaba**. Arabic and English can both be useful, depending on the participant. Football, food and travel are possible topics. Ask about an event only if they are interested; not everyone living in Qatar follows football.

### Bahrain
**Hala** is a simple greeting. Ask about music, food or a place visitors often overlook. If history interests both of you, Bahrain's connections with seafaring and pearling offer a direction for the conversation, without expecting everyone to be an expert.

### Oman
Start with **Marhaba**. Mountains, beaches, regional food and travel ideas are possible subjects. A respectful question such as “What would you recommend to a first-time visitor?” leaves the person free to answer without identifying their home.

### Jordan
Try **Marhaba, kifak?**. Mansaf, music, Petra or Wadi Rum might lead to a conversation. Ask whether the person has visited a place before requesting travel advice. Living in a country does not mean having seen every attraction.

### Lebanon
**Marhaba** is enough to begin. Arabic, French or English may suit your partner, but multilingual ability varies. Ask what they like listening to or cooking. If several languages appear in a sentence, ask for a short explanation rather than making assumptions.

### Iraq
An informal **Shlonak?** can be useful. Arabic and Kurdish are important languages, alongside other community languages. Ask which language they prefer. History, music, tea or a hobby can be openings; their location does not tell you their ethnicity or politics.

### Syria
Try **Marhaba, kifak?**. Food, music, a favourite book or a shared hobby can start a warm conversation. Do not demand personal stories about displacement or conflict. Let the other person decide which experiences they want to share.

## Europe and Türkiye: ask about the person first
### Türkiye
**Merhaba! Nasılsın?** means an informal hello and “How are you?” Turkish is a useful starting language. Ask for a series, song, food or travel recommendation. If your Turkish is basic, say so and check whether simple English or text translation would help.

### France
Start with **Bonjour !** and ask which language is easiest. Food, cinema, sport and travel can open the conversation. One polite French sentence shows effort, but it does not oblige the other person to switch to English or correct every mistake.

### Germany
**Hallo! Wie geht's?** is a familiar opening. Ask about music, a hobby, a walking route or a regional dish. A brief answer does not automatically signal unfriendliness; give the person space and ask a clear follow-up question.

### Italy
**Ciao! Come stai?** suits an informal chat. Regional food, music, football or a place worth visiting offer directions. “Which dish from your region should I try?” invites a personal recommendation without reducing Italy to a handful of clichés.

### Spain
Try **¡Hola! ¿Qué tal?** Spanish may be shared, while other languages also matter in different regions. Ask about a song, a festival or a favourite place. Check the person's actual free time instead of assuming everyone keeps the same schedule.

### United Kingdom
**Hi! How are you?** works well. There are many accents and several languages across the UK. Music, films, games, walking or sport can all work. If you miss a word, ask for a repeat; do not pretend an unfamiliar accent is incorrect English.

## The United States and India
### United States
An informal **Hey! How's it going?** is a possible opening. Do not assume everyone prefers English; ask. Films, hobbies, sport and travel are useful topics. The country spans multiple time zones, so a broad regional question is more practical than requesting a precise location.

### India
**Namaste** or **Hello** can start a conversation, depending on language preference. India is multilingual; Hindi is not everyone's first language, and English proficiency varies. Ask about films, music, food or games, then follow their interests instead of assuming cricket or Bollywood.

## Three questions that keep a conversation moving
1. What is one thing you like about the place where you live?
2. Is there a dish or song you would recommend to someone visiting your country?
3. What do you enjoy doing when you have a free afternoon?

Listen to the answer before moving on. If they mention cooking, ask what they like making. If they mention a game, ask how they got into it. Share a small non-identifying detail about yourself too, so the chat feels like an exchange rather than an interview.

## A small example, not a script to memorise
You might say, “Hallo! I'm learning a little German. Is simple English okay?” After the other person agrees, ask, “What music have you been listening to lately?” If they name a band, you can offer one recommendation in return. A greeting, a language check and a shared interest are enough to get started.

Use translation for short text messages and clarify anything that sounds harsh or confusing. Humour, slang and idioms can lose their meaning. “Could you say that another way?” is often more useful than assuming someone meant to be rude.

## Time zones and quiet country filters
There is no verified universal “best time” to meet users on Randly. Availability changes with actual traffic. Check local time and daylight-saving changes, and try more than one time window. Weekends, holidays and working patterns differ between people and countries.

If matching is slow, switch to global, relax very narrow interests or come back later. A filter cannot create participants who are not online. Do not interpret a quiet queue as proof that the camera is broken or that an entire country is unavailable.

## Privacy belongs in international conversations too
Keep your exact address, phone number, account codes and workplace private. Do not send money, follow unknown links or let a stranger pressure you into another app. A shared language and a convincing flag do not establish trust. ## Frequently asked questions
### Can I choose a country without paying?
Country preferences are available free on Randly. Successful matching still depends on available participants and the matching rules.

### Do I need to speak the other person's language?
No, but ask what works for both of you. Simple shared language and text translation can help; neither guarantees understanding or translates a live voice call.

### Does the flag verify citizenship?
No. It represents an estimated connection location and may be affected by routing, a VPN or missing geolocation data.

### What if I am shy?
Try text first, prepare one question and keep the first conversation short. You can end it politely whenever you want.

### Can I chat from a phone?
Use a supported modern browser. Video needs camera and microphone permission; text is an option when you prefer not to use the camera.

### Is everyone I meet a real person?
Do not assume that every interaction on an online service verifies a person's identity. Pay attention to the session's labels and disclosures, including any automated-chat notices. You can leave an interaction that does not suit you.
